# P6. People, Teams and Conflict

The People domain is 33% of the exam — about 56 scored questions — and it is the
domain where your CISSP gives you the least help and, worse, actively points you
at the wrong answer. This is the lesson to spend real time on.

Everything here is testable as a matching question as well as a scenario, so
learn the named models properly rather than approximately.

## Conflict resolution — learn this cold

Five modes. The exam tests them by name *and* by scenario, and there is a clear
ranking of which PMI wants.

| Mode | Outcome | What it is | When it is right |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Collaborate / problem solve** | **Win / win** | Incorporate multiple viewpoints, find the solution that serves everyone | **Almost always. This is the default answer.** |
| **Compromise / reconcile** | Lose / lose | Everyone gives up something to reach a solution all parties can accept | Second choice, when collaboration is not available in the time you have |
| **Smooth / accommodate** | Lose / win | Concede your position to preserve the relationship | Only under a real power imbalance — placating a sponsor, for instance |
| **Force / direct** | Win / lose | Impose your position | Genuine emergencies and crises. Needs positional authority. |
| **Withdraw / avoid** | — | Retreat from the conflict, defer it, let others resolve it | Rarely. Sometimes to prepare, or when the issue is genuinely not yours |

**The ranking to apply:** problem solve first, compromise next. Accommodate only
under extreme power imbalance. Force only in a crisis. Withdraw is almost never
the credited answer.

The trap: a question describes a heated disagreement and offers "escalate to the
sponsor", "make the decision yourself", "ask them to compromise", and "meet with
both parties to understand each position". The last one is collaboration, and it
wins — even though the first three all feel more decisive. **Decisiveness is not
the virtue this exam rewards.**

## Tuckman's ladder

How a team develops. Comes up constantly, usually as "which stage is this team
in?"

| Stage | What you see |
| --- | --- |
| **Forming** | Members come together, still independent and individual, polite, unclear on roles |
| **Storming** | Conflict as different personalities and working styles collide. **Normal and necessary.** |
| **Norming** | Roles understood, a working rhythm emerges, ground rules stick |
| **Performing** | High trust, interdependence, the team solves its own problems |
| **Adjourning** | The work finishes and the team disperses |

Two exam points. **Storming is expected, not a failure** — a question describing
a newly formed team in conflict usually wants you to recognise the stage and
support the team through it, not to intervene heavily or reassign people. And
teams can regress: adding members can drop a performing team back to forming.

## Motivation theory

Named models, matched to descriptions.

| Model | The idea |
| --- | --- |
| **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** | Needs are met bottom to top — physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. A level cannot be reached until the one below is met. |
| **Herzberg's two-factor theory** | **Hygiene factors** (salary fairness, job security, conditions, status) do not motivate — they only cause dissatisfaction when absent. **Motivators** (recognition, achievement, meaningful work) are what actually motivate. Fix hygiene first, then motivate. |
| **McGregor's Theory X / Theory Y** | **X**: people avoid work, need hands-on authoritarian management. **Y**: people take pride in their work, need participative management and trust. PMI prefers Y. |
| **Parkinson's Law** | Work expands to fill the time allotted. Hence short timeboxes. |
| **Student syndrome** | People start as late as possible, so padded estimates get consumed anyway. |

Herzberg is the one most often tested by scenario: *"the team got a pay rise and
morale still has not improved"* is a hygiene-versus-motivator question, and the
answer involves recognition or meaningful work, not more money.

## Leadership styles

| Style | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Servant leader** | Focuses on others' growth, learning, autonomy, and wellbeing. Removes impediments. **PMI's preferred style, especially in agile.** |
| **Transformational** | Inspirational motivation, raises the team's sights |
| **Transactional** | Rewards those who perform; manages by exception, stepping in where things go wrong |
| **Charismatic** | High energy, self-confident, strong convictions |
| **Laissez-faire** | Hands off; lets the team decide |
| **Autocratic** | One person has the final say (a product owner on backlog order, a sponsor on funding) |
| **Distributed** | The team self-organises and defines how it works |

Situational reading: a **new or uncertain team** needs more direction; an
**experienced, high-performing team** needs autonomy. Laissez-faire with a team
that is floundering is neglect; heavy direction with a performing team is
micromanagement. The exam will describe the team's state and expect you to match.

## Communication

Communication is the largest single use of a project manager's time, and PMI will
say so in an answer option.

**Channels**: `n(n − 1) / 2`. Ten people means 45 channels. If the question asks
for the *increase* when a team grows, calculate both and subtract — a favourite
trap.

**Push, pull, interactive:**

| Type | Examples | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Push** | Email, reports, memos, voicemail | Sending information out; no guarantee it was understood |
| **Pull** | Portals, wikis, databases, e-learning | Large volumes, accessed at the recipient's discretion |
| **Interactive** | Meetings, calls, conversations | Anything complex, sensitive, or needing confirmation of understanding |

For anything difficult, interactive wins. A question where a stakeholder is
upset and the options include "send a detailed report" and "meet with them" is
not a close call.

**Directions of influence** — a matching favourite:

| Direction | Who |
| --- | --- |
| **Upward** | Senior management, sponsor, steering committee |
| **Downward** | The project team, specialists contributing skills |
| **Sideward** | The PM's peers, other managers competing for the same resources |
| **Outward** | Suppliers, end users, government departments, the public |

**The five Cs of written communication**: correct grammar and spelling, concise
expression, clear purpose, coherent logical flow, controlling flow of words and
ideas.

**Communication richness** — a channel is rich when it carries multiple cues at
once (tone, body language), gives rapid feedback, is personal, and uses natural
language. Face-to-face is the richest; a written status report is the leanest.
That ranking is why agile prefers co-location and conversation over documents.

**Active listening** is engaging with the speaker, showing interest, and
summarising back to confirm understanding. When it appears as an option in a
conflict or stakeholder scenario, it is usually part of the right answer.

## Stakeholders — three of the eight People tasks

**The stakeholder register** holds: name, role on the project, power / interest /
influence, expectations and needs, communication requirements.

**Stakeholder engagement assessment matrix** — current (C) against desired (D)
engagement, on five levels: **unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading**.
When a question says a stakeholder has become resistant, this is the artefact
being asked for.

**Mapping models:**

| Model | Axes |
| --- | --- |
| Power / interest grid | Power against interest |
| Influence / impact grid | Influence on the project against impact by it |
| **Salience model** | Power, urgency, **legitimacy** — three inputs, so it is a cube rather than a grid |

Power/interest actions: high power + high interest → **manage closely**; high
power + low interest → **keep satisfied**; low power + high interest → **keep
informed**; low/low → **monitor**.

## Team artefacts

| Artefact | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Team charter** | Created *with the team*. Values, working agreements, ground rules, operating guidelines. Not the same as the project charter. |
| **Resource breakdown structure (RBS)** | Hierarchical chart of the team and physical resources |
| **Resource calendars** | When resources are actually available, holidays included |
| **RACI matrix** | A responsibility assignment matrix: who is **R**esponsible, **A**ccountable, **C**onsulted, **I**nformed per work package |

RACI rule that gets tested: **exactly one A per row.** Accountability does not
split.

## Who is accountable for what

A short list that resolves a surprising number of questions:

| Role | Accountable for |
| --- | --- |
| **Sponsor** | Funding, initiating the project, issuing the **project charter**, signing off and accepting deliverables |
| **Project manager** | Ensuring the work gets done; ensuring team members are adequately trained |
| **The team** | Estimating their own work, and performing it |
| **Vendor** | Their deliverable, as defined in the **contract** |
| **Functional manager** | Providing resources from their business unit |

When a vendor's deliverable is in trouble, the vendor is responsible for it — you
can help problem-solve, but you do not take over their accountability, and the
contract is what you check first.

## The People rules, condensed

1. **Deliver value above everything else.**
2. **Be direct and collaborative.** Go to the person, the team, or where the
   issue actually is.
3. **Be honest, take responsibility, do not pass the buck.**
4. **Problem solve before you escalate.** Collaborate with the sponsor, but try
   to solve it first.
5. **Use the right expertise** — the team for their way of working and their
   estimates, the customer for requirements and feedback. This is *expert
   judgement*.
6. **Do not suspend the project, add resources, or delay items** if you can solve
   the problem instead.

## The root-cause routine

Many People questions are really problem-solving questions. The sequence:

> Define the problem → identify the **root cause** → brainstorm solutions →
> prioritise and choose → implement → **verify it worked**

Two things follow. **Find the root cause before spending any money** — an answer
that throws budget or people at a symptom loses to one that diagnoses. And make
sure you are solving the *right* problem: the question's keywords will tell you
whether it is a team problem or a supplier problem, and those have different
answers.

If a question uses a term you have never seen, do not panic and do not guess at
the term. Go back to the keywords and pick the answer that addresses the root
cause. Unfamiliar vocabulary is often the distractor itself.

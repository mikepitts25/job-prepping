# P6. Drill Set: Forty Questions

These are written in the exam's style and, more importantly, each one comes with
the reasoning rather than just a letter. Work them with the four-step routine
from [P3](pmp-mindset.html): stem verb, development approach, where you are,
eliminate.

Cover the answers. Commit to a choice before reading on. Grading yourself on
reasoning is the point — a question you got right for the wrong reason is a
question you will get wrong next time in a different costume.

## A. People (1–10)

**1.** A newly formed team is producing inconsistent work and members are unclear
who decides what. What should the project manager do first?  
*A.* Assign a technical lead with decision authority.  
*B.* Facilitate a session to agree team ground rules and working agreements.  
*C.* Escalate to the functional managers for clearer role definitions.  
*D.* Increase status reporting frequency to catch problems earlier.

> **B.** New team, no shared norms — the People domain's "build a shared vision
> and team culture" task. A imposes a solution without involving the team. C
> escalates before trying the direct route. D treats the symptom.

**2.** Two senior engineers disagree sharply about an architecture decision in
front of the whole team. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Make the decision to restore momentum.  
*B.* Ask the sponsor to arbitrate.  
*C.* Meet with both engineers to understand each position and facilitate agreement.  
*D.* Have the team vote.

> **C.** Conflict resolved at the lowest level, by the people involved, with the
> PM facilitating. A is the PM deciding a technical matter that belongs to the
> team. B escalates prematurely. D avoids the substance.

**3.** A high-performing developer has started missing daily standups and
delivering late. What should the PM do first?  
*A.* Document the performance issue and inform HR.  
*B.* Reassign the developer's critical work to someone else.  
*C.* Have a private conversation with the developer.  
*D.* Raise it in the retrospective so the team can address it.

> **C.** Understand before acting, and never discipline or surface an individual
> publicly. D is worse than it looks — naming a person's performance in a
> retrospective is exactly the blame behaviour PMI rules out.

**4.** The team is co-located except for two members in another time zone who
consistently seem out of the loop. What is the best response?  
*A.* Ask them to shift their hours to overlap with the core team.  
*B.* Revise the communications management plan to ensure inclusive channels and timing.  
*C.* Record all meetings and share them afterwards.  
*D.* Move their work to the co-located team members.

> **B.** "Best" wants the most complete answer. C is a partial fix, A pushes the
> cost onto them without analysis, D removes them rather than including them. B
> addresses the plan that governs all of it.

**5.** A stakeholder who was supportive at kickoff has become openly critical in
steering meetings. What should the PM do first?  
*A.* Update the stakeholder engagement assessment matrix and meet with them.  
*B.* Ask the sponsor to speak to them.  
*C.* Provide more detailed status reports to the whole steering group.  
*D.* Remove them from the distribution list to reduce disruption.

> **A.** Engagement changed from supportive to resistant — the matrix is the
> artefact for exactly that, and the meeting is the direct route. D is
> indefensible and shows up more often than you would think.

**6.** The only engineer who understands a legacy interface is retiring in two
months. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Document the risk in the risk register and monitor it.  
*B.* Plan and begin structured knowledge transfer to at least two other engineers.  
*C.* Offer a retention bonus to delay the retirement.  
*D.* Contract the engineer for support after retirement.

> **B.** Manage knowledge transfer is an ECO task in its own right. A is passive
> when action is available; C and D postpone the problem instead of removing it.

**7.** During sprint planning the team says they cannot commit to the scope the
product owner wants. What should the Scrum Master do?  
*A.* Ask the team to commit and work overtime if needed.  
*B.* Facilitate a conversation so the product owner and developers agree on a realistic sprint goal.  
*C.* Escalate to the project sponsor.  
*D.* Extend the sprint by one week.

> **B.** Servant leadership: facilitate, do not direct. The developers decide how
> much they take. D breaks the fixed timebox — always wrong.

**8.** A team member tells the PM privately that another member is taking credit
for their work. What should the PM do first?  
*A.* Confront the other team member.  
*B.* Ask questions to understand the situation fully.  
*C.* Raise it as a team issue in the next retrospective.  
*D.* Adjust the work assignments so their contributions are visible.

> **B.** One-sided account, no verification yet. Gather information first. Every
> other option acts on an unverified claim.

**9.** A functional manager pulls a team member onto other work without telling
the PM. What is the best first action?  
*A.* Escalate to the sponsor immediately.  
*B.* Discuss the impact with the functional manager and seek a resolution.  
*C.* Re-plan the schedule around the reduced capacity.  
*D.* Submit a change request for additional resources.

> **B.** Direct route first. This is textbook matrix-organisation friction, and
> PMI wants negotiation with the functional manager before escalation. If it
> fails, *then* A.

**10.** A project manager notices the team's retrospectives have become
perfunctory, with the same items raised and never resolved. What should they do?  
*A.* Stop running retrospectives since they add no value.  
*B.* Change the retrospective format and ensure each retro produces one owned, actionable improvement.  
*C.* Run the retrospectives themselves instead of the Scrum Master.  
*D.* Report the team's lack of engagement to management.

> **B.** Drive continuous improvement. A abandons inspection and adaptation; C
> takes the Scrum Master's accountability; D blames.

## B. Process (11–24)

**11.** A stakeholder asks for a small additional feature directly to a developer,
who builds it in an afternoon. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Nothing, since the cost was negligible.  
*B.* Thank the developer for the responsiveness.  
*C.* Address the scope change through change control and remind the team of the process.  
*D.* Remove the feature immediately.

> **C.** Gold plating plus an out-of-process change. Size does not exempt it. D
> is also out-of-process — you do not unilaterally remove delivered work either.

**12.** BAC is $800,000. EV is $300,000, AC is $400,000, PV is $360,000. What is
the EAC assuming current performance continues?  
*A.* $900,000 *B.* $1,066,667 *C.* $700,000 *D.* $1,200,000

> **B.** `CPI = 300,000 / 400,000 = 0.75`. `EAC = BAC / CPI = 800,000 / 0.75 =
> $1,066,667`. A is `AC + (BAC − EV)`, the one-off-variance formula — correct
> arithmetic, wrong assumption, and the most common trap in this family.

**13.** Same figures. What is the SPI, and what does it mean?  
*A.* 0.75, over budget *B.* 0.83, behind schedule *C.* 1.20, ahead of schedule  
*D.* 0.83, over budget

> **B.** `SPI = EV / PV = 300,000 / 360,000 = 0.83`. Division by PV is schedule;
> below 1.0 is behind. D pairs the right number with the wrong dimension, which
> is how these questions catch people who memorised the formula but not what it
> measures.

**14.** A project's critical path is 40 days. An activity not on the critical
path with 6 days of total float slips by 3 days. What is the effect on the
project end date?  
*A.* 3 days late *B.* No effect *C.* 6 days late *D.* Cannot be determined

> **B.** The slip is inside the float. Note the follow-on trap: the activity's
> remaining total float is now 3 days, so a further slip of more than 3 would
> delay the project.

**15.** The sponsor wants delivery four weeks earlier and will fund additional
resources but will not accept additional risk. What should the PM recommend?  
*A.* Fast-track the critical path. *B.* Crash the critical path.  
*C.* Reduce scope. *D.* Reduce quality standards.

> **B.** Crashing buys time with money. Fast-tracking buys it with risk, which
> was ruled out. C is a change request, not compression. D is never an answer.

**16.** Optimistic 8 days, most likely 14 days, pessimistic 32 days. What is the
PERT estimate?  
*A.* 18 *B.* 16 *C.* 14.67 *D.* 20

> **B.** `(8 + 4×14 + 32) / 6 = 96 / 6 = 16`. A is the triangular average
> `(8+14+32)/3 = 18`, offered to catch the wrong formula.

**17.** Requirements for a new subsystem are not yet defined, and the work is
developmental. Which contract type is most appropriate?  
*A.* Firm fixed price *B.* Cost plus fixed fee  
*C.* Fixed price incentive fee *D.* Fixed price with economic price adjustment

> **B.** Undefined scope means the buyer must carry the risk. Every fixed-price
> variant requires definable scope to price.

**18.** A deliverable passed the team's internal inspection but the customer
refuses to accept it. Which process failed?  
*A.* Control quality *B.* Validate scope *C.* Manage quality *D.* Define scope

> **D.** Control quality succeeded — it was internally correct. Validate scope is
> where the refusal *surfaced*, not where the failure originated. If it was built
> to requirements and still rejected, the requirements were wrong. This is a
> "best" question wearing a "which" costume; read carefully.

**19.** Halfway through execution, the PM realises a work package in the schedule
is not in the WBS. What does this indicate?  
*A.* Normal progressive elaboration. *B.* A violation of the 100% rule requiring scope baseline correction through change control. *C.* Gold plating.  
*D.* The WBS dictionary needs updating.

> **B.** The WBS must contain 100% of the scope. Work outside it is either
> out-of-scope work being done, or a baseline defect — either way it goes through
> change control.

**20.** The team has completed 40% of the work, spent 45% of the budget, and is
on schedule. What is true?  
*A.* CPI > 1 and SPI = 1 *B.* CPI < 1 and SPI = 1  
*C.* CPI = 1 and SPI < 1 *D.* Insufficient information

> **B.** More money spent than value earned means AC > EV, so CPI < 1. On
> schedule means EV = PV, so SPI = 1. You can reason all of this without a single
> currency figure, which is the skill the question is testing.

**21.** A risk that was identified and had a response plan has now occurred. What
should the PM do?  
*A.* Update the risk register with the new probability.  
*B.* Execute the planned risk response and record the event in the issue log.  
*C.* Submit a change request to add contingency reserve.  
*D.* Perform qualitative risk analysis on the event.

> **B.** It happened, so it is an issue. Execute the plan you already made and
> log it. A and D are risk-register activities for things that have not happened.

**22.** During sprint review a stakeholder requests a significant new feature.
What should happen?  
*A.* Submit a change request to the change control board.  
*B.* The product owner adds it to the product backlog and prioritises it.  
*C.* Add it to the current sprint since the team has capacity.  
*D.* Reject it as out of scope.

> **B.** Agile scenario — the signal words are "sprint review" and the presence
> of a product owner. A applies predictive machinery to an agile context, which
> is the single most tested flip on the exam. C injects work mid-sprint.

**23.** A project's TCPI to the original BAC is 1.35. What does this tell the
project manager?  
*A.* The project is ahead of budget.  
*B.* Remaining work must be performed 35% more efficiently than planned to meet the original budget.  
*C.* The project will overrun by 35%.  
*D.* The schedule must be compressed by 35%.

> **B.** TCPI is the required future efficiency. Above 1.0 means harder than
> planned; 1.35 against a track record below 1.0 means the original budget is
> not realistically recoverable, and the honest move is a conversation with the
> sponsor, not a heroic plan.

**24.** The customer has been using the delivered system in production for six
weeks but has not signed the acceptance document. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Consider the project closed, since the product is in use.  
*B.* Escalate to the sponsor to force sign-off.  
*C.* Meet the customer to identify what is preventing formal acceptance, then resolve it.  
*D.* Issue the final report and release the team.

> **C.** Use is not acceptance. Understand the blocker first — there is usually a
> real reason, often an unmet acceptance criterion. B escalates before the direct
> route; A and D close a project that is not closed.

## C. Business Environment (25–34)

**25.** Midway through a project, a new data-protection regulation takes effect in
the customer's country. What should the PM do first?  
*A.* Continue as planned; the requirement was not in the baseline.  
*B.* Assess the impact of the regulation on the project and raise a change request if required.  
*C.* Immediately implement the necessary changes.  
*D.* Inform the sponsor that the project must be cancelled.

> **B.** Monitor and evaluate external changes, then the change chain. C
> implements without impact analysis and without approval, however obviously
> necessary the change might be.

**26.** An organisation has no defined escalation path for decisions above the
PM's authority. What does the project lack?  
*A.* A communications plan *B.* Project governance  
*C.* A stakeholder register *D.* A RACI matrix

> **B.** Decision rights and escalation paths are the definition of governance.
> A RACI is a component of it, not the whole thing.

**27.** A supplier's manufacturing process has significant environmental impact
that was not considered during source selection. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Nothing; the contract is already awarded.  
*B.* Assess it as a compliance and sustainability risk and take it through risk and change processes.  
*C.* Terminate the contract.  
*D.* Note it in lessons learned for future projects.

> **B.** Sustainability became explicitly examinable in the 2026 outline. Do not
> read an environmental option as a soft distractor any more — here it is the
> live one. D is the passive version and defers the whole problem.

**28.** The PM discovers that a status report sent to the customer overstated
progress. What should they do?  
*A.* Correct it in the next reporting cycle.  
*B.* Notify the customer promptly with the corrected information.  
*C.* Investigate who prepared the report and address it internally.  
*D.* Adjust the remaining plan so the reported figures become true.

> **B.** Honesty and promptness. D is the interesting wrong answer — it is what
> pressure actually produces in the field, and it is a Code of Ethics violation.

**29.** An organisation is adopting a new tool that the project will deliver, but
end users are resisting. Whose concern is this?  
*A.* Not the project's; adoption is an operational matter.  
*B.* The project manager's, under supporting organisational change.  
*C.* The sponsor's alone.  
*D.* The change control board's.

> **B.** Support organisational change is an ECO task. Benefits realisation
> depends on adoption, so it sits inside the project's concern even when it
> lands after handover.

**30.** A team proposes using a generative AI tool to draft test cases from
requirements. What should the PM consider first?  
*A.* The productivity gain.  
*B.* Whether the use complies with organisational policy, contractual terms, and data handling requirements.  
*C.* Whether the team has the necessary training.  
*D.* The cost of the tool.

> **B.** AI is on the 2026 outline mostly as a governance and compliance topic.
> This is your CISSP reflex and here it is exactly right — data classification
> and third-party handling before benefit.

**31.** A risk has been identified that is outside the project's authority to
address and belongs to the wider organisation. What is the correct response?  
*A.* Accept *B.* Mitigate *C.* Escalate *D.* Transfer

> **C.** Escalate is a valid response for both threats and opportunities. Once
> escalated and accepted by the receiving party, it leaves the project's risk
> register.

**32.** Which is a *management* reserve rather than a contingency reserve?  
*A.* Budget set aside for identified risks in the register.  
*B.* Budget held outside the cost baseline for unforeseen work within scope.  
*C.* Schedule float on the critical path.  
*D.* Budget allocated to a specific work package for rework.

> **B.** Contingency is for known risks, inside the cost baseline, PM-controlled.
> Management reserve is for unknown unknowns, outside the cost baseline, and
> needs management approval to use. C is a nonsense option — the critical path
> has zero float by definition.

**33.** Halfway through delivery, a competitor releases a product that makes part
of the planned scope obsolete. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Complete the scope as baselined.  
*B.* Evaluate the business case's continued validity and bring findings to the sponsor and governance body.  
*C.* Remove the affected scope from the plan.  
*D.* Cancel the project.

> **B.** External change plus governance. The project exists to deliver benefit;
> when the benefit case changes, that goes to whoever owns the business case. C
> changes the baseline unilaterally; D is not the PM's call.

**34.** An organisation runs quarterly lessons-learned workshops at project
closure only. What improvement should the PM recommend?  
*A.* Running them more frequently at closure.  
*B.* Capturing lessons continuously in a lessons learned register throughout the project, feeding the repository at closure.  
*C.* Assigning a dedicated lessons-learned coordinator.  
*D.* Making attendance mandatory.

> **B.** Lessons are captured continuously — the register during, the repository
> after. Closure-only capture loses everything learned early, when memory is
> freshest and the improvement could still have helped this project.

## D. Mixed and graphic-style (35–40)

**35.** A burnup chart shows the "total scope" line rising steadily over six
sprints while the "completed" line rises at a constant rate. What is happening?  
*A.* The team is slowing down. *B.* Scope is growing.  
*C.* The team is ahead of schedule. *D.* Velocity is increasing.

> **B.** The total-scope line is exactly what a burnup adds over a burndown, and
> a rising one is scope growth. The team's rate is unchanged; the finish line is
> moving.

**36.** A cumulative flow diagram shows the "in test" band widening steadily while
other bands stay constant. What should the team do?  
*A.* Add more developers. *B.* Address the bottleneck in testing, e.g. by limiting WIP upstream or shifting capacity to testing.  
*C.* Increase the sprint length. *D.* Reduce the definition of done.

> **B.** A widening band is a bottleneck in that state. A makes it worse by
> pushing more work into the queue. D lowers the quality bar to hide it.

**37.** A team of 6 grows to 10. By how much does the number of communication
channels increase?  
*A.* 4 *B.* 15 *C.* 30 *D.* 45

> **C.** `6×5/2 = 15`, `10×9/2 = 45`, increase = 30. D is the new total, offered
> because the question asks for the increase. Read the question.

**38.** A project manager in a weak matrix organisation has been asked to deliver
a project but has no authority over the resources. What is the most significant
implication?  
*A.* They cannot be held accountable for the outcome.  
*B.* Negotiation with functional managers becomes a primary and ongoing activity.  
*C.* The project should be run as agile.  
*D.* A project coordinator should be appointed instead.

> **B.** In a weak matrix the PM's power is influence, not authority.
> Negotiation for resources is continuous, and that is what the organisational
> structure questions are really testing.

**39.** During execution, quality control charts show seven consecutive
measurements above the mean, all within control limits. What should the PM do?  
*A.* Nothing; the process is in control.  
*B.* Investigate, since seven consecutive points on one side of the mean indicates a non-random trend.  
*C.* Stop the process and recalibrate.  
*D.* Widen the control limits.

> **B.** The rule of seven. Inside the limits but non-random means something has
> changed, and investigating before it breaches is the proactive answer. D is
> fabricating a pass.

**40.** A project is being delivered under a firm fixed price contract with fully
defined scope, under regulatory certification, by a distributed team across three
contractors. Which development approach is most appropriate?  
*A.* Scrum *B.* Predictive, possibly with agile practices inside delivery teams  
*C.* Kanban *D.* Extreme Programming

> **B.** Every factor in the stem — fixed price, fixed scope, certification,
> contractual interfaces — points predictive. The "possibly with agile practices
> inside" clause is what makes B more complete than a bare predictive option, and
> the 2026 exam rewards that hybrid framing.

## Grading yourself

For every one you got wrong, write a single line naming the cause:

- misread the stem verb (first vs next vs best)
- missed the development approach signal
- treated an issue as a risk, or vice versa
- acted without gathering information
- went around a process
- arithmetic or wrong formula variant

After forty questions you will have a list of about four recurring causes. Those,
not the topics, are what to drill. Then go get a realistic full-length mock — PMI
Study Hall is the one built directly against the current outline — and repeat the
same grading discipline on 180 questions.

P1. PMP: What You Are Signing Up For

You have a CISSP. That is worth saying up front, because it changes what this lesson needs to cover. You already know how a body-of-knowledge certification works, you already know how to study a domain outline, and you already know the feeling of an exam that asks what you should do rather than what you could do. All of that transfers.

What does not transfer is the shape of the content, and one specific habit that will cost you marks if you bring it across unexamined. More on that in P3.

The thirty-second version
Issuer Project Management Institute (PMI)
Exam version New outline live since 9 July 2026
Questions 180 total (170 scored, 10 unscored pretest)
Time 240 minutes, with two 10-minute breaks
Domains People 33%, Process 41%, Business Environment 26%
Delivery Pearson VUE test centre or online proctored
Result Pass/fail, with a per-domain band (Above Target / Target / Below Target / Needs Improvement)
Renewal 60 PDUs every 3 years

There is no published numeric pass mark. PMI scores psychometrically, so the honest answer to "what percentage do I need" is "nobody outside PMI knows". People who consistently score in the high 70s on realistic mock exams pass.

Am I eligible?

Two routes. You need the education hours and one of the experience rows.

Your education Project-leading experience required
Four-year degree (bachelor's or global equivalent) 36 months
High school diploma, associate degree, or global equivalent 60 months

Plus 35 contact hours of formal project management education — or a current CAPM, which substitutes for the 35 hours.

Two things people get wrong:

"Leading projects" is broader than "being called a project manager." PMI's own wording is about leading and directing project work. A product owner running a delivery backlog, a technical lead owning scope and schedule for a workstream, an engineer who ran an integration effort end to end — all of that counts, if you can describe it in PMI's language. Your ADOC product owner time is squarely eligible experience.

The months do not need to be full-time, but they do not double-count. Overlapping projects in the same month count as one month.

The application, and the audit

The application asks for each project: dates, your role, the organisation, and a short description of what you did, written in terms of what you led — initiating, planning, executing, monitoring, closing. Write those descriptions in PMI's vocabulary. Not "I ran the sprint board" but "Led requirements definition and backlog prioritisation with stakeholders, managed the delivery schedule across two vendor teams, and reported status and risk to the programme board."

Roughly one application in ten is randomly audited. An audit asks for a signature from a manager or client who can verify each project, plus proof of your degree and your 35 contact hours. Get the contact details of your verifiers before you apply, not after — chasing a former manager in Qatar while a 90-day audit clock runs is the avoidable version of this problem.

Once approved, you have one year and up to three attempts to sit the exam.

Join PMI first. The membership costs less than the fee difference it saves, and it unlocks free digital copies of the PMBOK Guide and the other PMI standards, which is the single biggest free-material lever available to you. Do the maths once and it is obvious; do not skip it.

What the exam actually feels like

Not like CISSP. CISSP asks a lot of "which of these is the best definition" and "which control addresses this". PMP asks almost entirely situational questions:

A project is midway through execution. A key stakeholder who has been quiet since kickoff emails the sponsor to say the delivered increments do not match what they expected. What should the project manager do first?

Four answers, all of them things a competent person might do, one of them the thing PMI believes you should do first. That "first / next / best" framing is the whole exam. Memorising definitions gets you maybe 20% of the paper.

The 2026 version adds two question formats on top of the familiar ones:

  • Case or scenario questions — a longer narrative followed by several questions about the same situation, sat in their own timed section at the start of the exam.
  • Graphic-based questions — a chart, burndown, network diagram, or EVM table that you read to answer.

Alongside these you will still see multiple choice, multiple response ("choose three"), matching, drag-and-drop, and point-and-click hotspot items. Multiple response questions tell you how many to pick and give no partial credit.

What changed in July 2026, and why it matters to you

The headline is the reweighting. Business Environment went from 8% to 26% of the exam. That domain is governance, compliance, risk, change control, organisational change, continuous improvement, and scanning the external environment.

Read that list again. Governance, compliance, and risk are CISSP home turf. You are walking into an exam whose fastest-growing domain is the one your existing certification already trained you for. That is a genuine advantage and you should plan around it: spend your scarce study hours on People and Process, and treat Business Environment as a translation exercise from vocabulary you already own.

The other changes worth knowing:

  • Tasks were consolidated, not added: People went 14 → 8, Process 17 → 10, Business Environment 4 → 8. Twenty-six tasks total, down from 35.
  • Sustainability is now explicitly examinable, woven into compliance and risk.
  • AI in project management appears as a topic — using it, governing it, and the ethics of it.
  • The exam now leans on the PMBOK Guide 8th edition, which consolidated the 7th edition's 12 principles into 6 and its 8 performance domains into 7, with 40 processes embedded in them, and added a chapter on artificial intelligence. P4 is the map of it.

You do not sit the exam on the PMBOK Guide. You sit it on the Exam Content Outline. PMBOK is a reference, not a syllabus — a distinction PMI has made repeatedly and that candidates keep ignoring.

How long this takes

For someone with real delivery experience and a proven ability to study for a professional exam, 8 to 12 weeks at 6 to 8 hours a week is realistic. Faster is possible and usually means worse retention of the situational judgment, which is the part you cannot cram.

P10 lays that out week by week.

The order to do things in

  1. Join PMI (~$139 all in). Download the free ECO and the PMBOK Guide.
  2. Book your 35 contact hours course. Do not start studying seriously before this; the course is also your structure.
  3. Write the application while the course runs, and line up your verifiers.
  4. Submit. Approval is usually days; an audit adds weeks.
  5. Book the exam date as soon as you are approved. A date on the calendar is the single most reliable study accelerant there is.
  6. Study. Mock exams from week 6.
